# Call — Beacon Charter Schools × BetterUp · Program Review

Participants: [EXTERNAL] Marcus Webb, Director of Leadership Development (Beacon Charter Schools) · [INTERNAL] Tomás Vela, CSM

[INTERNAL] Tomás: Marcus, good morning. School's out for summer, right? You sound relaxed.
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: We wrapped last Friday. I can hear birds again. During the year my inbox is a war zone, so this is my favorite six weeks.
[INTERNAL] Tomás: Enjoy it while it lasts. Before we get into it — how many schools are you up to now? Last I remember you were opening a couple.
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: Eleven campuses now. We opened two this year, which is its own kind of madness. Standing up a new school is like launching a startup that's legally responsible for children.
[INTERNAL] Tomás: That's a terrifying framing and I suspect entirely accurate.
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: Accurate to the bone. Anyway, the two new campuses have first-year principals, which is exactly the population coaching was built for — people in over their heads who won't admit it.
[INTERNAL] Tomás: We'll come back to that, because new-principal support is a great story. So this is our program review — I mostly wanted to hear how the principal cohort went this year and celebrate what worked. Less "let's fix things," more "let's take stock."
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: Music to my ears, because honestly it went really well and I don't say that lightly. I'm a skeptic by trade.
[INTERNAL] Tomás: Tell me. What worked?
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: So, context. We put 55 school leaders through it — principals, assistant principals, a few instructional coaches. This is a brutally hard job. Turnover in charter leadership is savage. People burn out in three years and leave education entirely.
[INTERNAL] Tomás: Right, the churn is the whole problem.
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: It's the whole problem. And my hypothesis going in was that coaching might help people feel less alone in the role. That's it. Modest goal. Just — don't quit.
[INTERNAL] Tomás: And?
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: And it exceeded that. We had two principals who told me, unprompted, in exit-of-year conversations, that their coach was the reason they didn't resign in February. February's the killer month, by the way. Everyone wants to quit in February.
[INTERNAL] Tomás: That's a real outcome, Marcus. Two people staying in the profession is not a soft metric.
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: It's the only metric I actually care about. Everything else is a proxy for "did good people stay and keep doing hard work well."
[INTERNAL] Tomás: How did engagement look across the cohort? Just so we have the numbers on the record.
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: You'd know better than me, but from my dashboard it looked like most people were consistent. There were a handful who never really engaged, which is fine, you can't save everyone. But the middle and top were solid.
[INTERNAL] Tomás: That matches what I see — about 80% had regular sessions through the year, which for a group as time-starved as school leaders is frankly excellent. Principals don't have free hours.
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: They have negative free hours. The fact that anyone showed up is a miracle. I think the confidentiality mattered. A principal can't be vulnerable with their superintendent or their staff. The coach was the one safe room.
[INTERNAL] Tomás: That comes up constantly in leadership contexts — the higher you go, the fewer people you can be honest with.
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: Lonely at the top, even when the top is a school of 400 kids and a leaky roof.
[INTERNAL] Tomás: Especially then. Did the new-campus principals get into it, or were they too underwater to engage?
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: Surprisingly, they engaged the hardest. I think when you're drowning you grab any life raft offered. One of them — first-year principal, inherited a mess — told me the coaching was the only thing keeping her sane in the first ninety days.
[INTERNAL] Tomás: That's exactly the moment coaching earns its keep. First ninety days of a hard new role.
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: And it's the moment we'd historically abandoned people. "Congratulations on the promotion, good luck, figure it out." The coaching filled a gap we didn't even admit existed.
[INTERNAL] Tomás: So what made it work operationally, from your seat? Anything you'd want other education customers to know?
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: Two things. One, we didn't make it mandatory. The second you mandate development for educators they treat it like another compliance box. We made it a perk. "You've earned access to an executive coach." Framing is everything.
[INTERNAL] Tomás: That's a great insight. The voluntary framing.
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: Two, we protected the time. I told my principals explicitly: this is not something you do at 9pm after everything else. Block it in your calendar like a board meeting. And I modeled it — I did my own coaching and talked about it openly.
[INTERNAL] Tomás: Leaders who visibly use it themselves. That's the single biggest predictor of adoption we see.
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: I believe it. Nobody does what you say. They do what you do.
[INTERNAL] Tomás: Can I quote you on some of this? Anonymized or named, your call — but "the reason I didn't resign in February" is exactly the kind of thing that helps other education leaders understand what this can do.
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: You can quote me. I'll even do a reference call if it helps another district. I'm evangelical about this now, which is embarrassing for a former skeptic.
[INTERNAL] Tomás: The converted skeptics make the best references. Nobody trusts the person who loved everything from day one.
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: True. I was a hard sell. I told my predecessor this was probably a waste of money. I owe him an apology, wherever he is.
[INTERNAL] Tomás: I'll take the win. Was there a specific moment you flipped? I'm curious what turns a skeptic.
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: Honestly? It was watching a principal I'd nearly given up on turn her whole building around. She'd been struggling — staff morale in the basement, test scores sliding. Six months of coaching later she's running one of my strongest campuses. I can't prove causation, but I watched it happen in real time.
[INTERNAL] Tomás: You watched a leader grow into the job instead of washing out. That's not nothing, even without a controlled study.
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: It's everything, in my world. Replacing a principal mid-year is a catastrophe — it destabilizes an entire school. Keeping one and helping her succeed is worth more than I can put on a spreadsheet.
[INTERNAL] Tomás: That's the retention math again, just at a higher stakes level. So looking ahead — same size next year, or are you thinking about growing it?
[EXTERNAL] Marcus: Growing, probably. I want to reach down a level — the teacher-leaders, the department heads, the people who might become principals. Build the bench. But that's a fall conversation, once I know my budget from the board.
